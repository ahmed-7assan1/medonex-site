### Aim vs. Objectives

<details>
<summary>🎯 Aim</summary>
<div>

*   **Broad statement** describing the overall intention.
*   Uses action verbs: To assess, To evaluate, To investigate, To understand, To explore.
*   **Example:** <span style="color: #2563eb;">"This study is conducted to assess the nutritional problems among primary school children."</span>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>📋 Objectives (SMART)</summary>
<div>

*   **How** you will achieve the aims.
*   **Specific**, avoid vague statements.
*   Uses action verbs: To test, To determine, To develop, To design, To collect, To measure, To analyze.

<div class="callout callout-tip">
<strong>SMART Criteria:</strong>
</div>

<dl>
<dt>S — Specific</dt>
<dd>Clearly defined and focused</dd>
<dt>M — Measurable</dt>
<dd>Effect size can be quantified</dd>
<dt>A — Applicable / Achievable</dt>
<dd>Realistic within resources</dd>
<dt>R — Relevant</dt>
<dd>Realistic and meaningful to the field</dd>
<dt>T — Time-bound</dt>
<dd>Has a defined time frame &amp; end point</dd>
</dl>

</div>
</details>

### Descriptive Studies: "The First Toe in Water" 🦶

<div class="shape-circle">D</div>

<div class="callout callout-info">
<strong>Definition:</strong> First phase in epidemiological investigation; describes the <span style="color: #7c3aed;">pattern</span>, <span style="color: #7c3aed;">characteristics</span>, and <span style="color: #7c3aed;">distribution</span> of a disease or health problem in the population.
</div>

<div class="shape-diamond">
<strong>Focus:</strong> <span style="color: #dc2626;">When</span> (Time) · <span style="color: #2563eb;">Where</span> (Place) · <span style="color: #16a34a;">Who</span> (Person) is getting the disease.
</div>

<div class="callout callout-note">
<strong>Purpose:</strong> Formulating (not testing) research hypotheses — the 1st step in searching for determinants or risk factors.
</div>

---

### Uses of Descriptive Studies

<ol>
<li><strong>Providing data</strong> about the magnitude and distribution of health problems.</li>
<li><strong>Describing</strong> the natural history of diseases.</li>
<li><strong>Identifying</strong> risk groups.</li>
<li><strong>Formulating hypotheses</strong> for further analytical studies.</li>
<li><strong>Planning and evaluating</strong> health services.</li>
</ol>

### Types of Descriptive Studies

<details>
<summary>📝 1. Case Report</summary>
<div>

<div class="shape-tag">Single Case</div>

Detailed presentation of a **single case** reporting a new or unique finding (e.g., newly described disease, unexpected therapeutic effect).

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>📝 2. Case Series</summary>
<div>

<div class="shape-tag">Multiple Cases</div>

Describes a number of **similar cases** with a given disease in one report. May describe unusual variations or indicate the start of an epidemic. A major trigger for further research.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>📊 3. Ecological (Correlational) Studies</summary>
<div>

<div class="shape-tag">Population Level</div>

*   Looks for **associations (correlation)** between exposures &amp; outcomes in *populations* rather than individuals.
*   Uses already collected population data (e.g., vital statistics, censuses).
*   Compares populations in different places at the same time or the same population over different times.

<div class="callout callout-tip">
<strong>Examples:</strong>
* Mortality from CHD &amp; per capita cigarette sales
* High MI incidence &amp; dietary fat consumption
</div>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>📸 4. Cross-Sectional (Prevalence) Studies</summary>
<div>

<div class="shape-notch">

**Snapshot** — observational study carried out *once* at a **single point in time**.

Both exposure (risk factors) and outcome (diseases) are present simultaneously; **cannot determine** if exposure preceded disease.

Measures **prevalence**, not incidence.

</div>

<details>
<summary>✅ Uses</summary>
<div>

1.  Study prevalence rates of chronic diseases.
2.  Study disease load and its distribution in subgroups.
3.  Screening for unrecognized cases.
4.  Detection of association between risk factors &amp; diseases.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>👍 Advantages</summary>
<div>

1.  Useful for frequent, long-duration conditions.
2.  Good for generating hypotheses.
3.  Estimates prevalence rates and exposure proportions.
4.  No follow-up, relatively easy, quick, and inexpensive.
5.  First step to develop causal association.

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>👎 Disadvantages</summary>
<div>

<div class="callout callout-danger">
<strong>Limitations:</strong>
</div>

1.  Not useful for acute, seasonal, highly fatal, rare, or short-duration diseases.
2.  Cannot estimate incidence rate.
3.  Little information about natural history of diseases.
4.  Cannot determine if exposure preceded disease.
5.  Cannot differentiate between causes and associated factors.

</div>
</details>

</div>
</details>

<details>
<summary>📈 5. Longitudinal (Incidence) Studies</summary>
<div>

<div class="shape-asymmetric">

**Repeated observations (follow-up)** in the same community over a prolonged period to identify **new cases** of disease.

**Measures:**
1.  Incidence rate.
2.  Natural history of disease &amp; its final outcome (case fatality, survival).
3.  Risk factors of disease.

</div>

<div class="callout callout-danger">
<strong>Problems:</strong> Loss to follow-up, difficulty maintaining standards over time.
</div>

</div>
</details>

### Summary of Descriptive Studies

| Type | Scope | Key Feature |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Case Report** | One case | Unusual disease/injury finding |
| **Case Series** | Multiple cases | Similar disease/injury pattern |
| **Correlation** | Entire population | Aggregated data associations |
| **Cross-Sectional** | Cross-section snapshot | <span style="color: #2563eb;">**Prevalence**</span> at one point in time |
| **Longitudinal** | Follow-up over time | <span style="color: #dc2626;">**Incidence**</span> — new cases identified |
